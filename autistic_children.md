## Introduction

As a parent or guardian of an autistic child, you might be worried that he or she is not eating enough vitamins and micronutrients, since he or she is a picky eater. Your child only eats a few foods, but at least your child is eating. There is no reason to feel guilty here, since the government of your country should be providing you with real help and guidance about how you can help your child get enough good nutrients beyond protein and sugar. However, as in many other cases where governments should be doing something to help the population, the effort is limited to good intentions, and they put little effort into deploying them to parents and guardians. Some researchers have tried to do something as well, such as working with parents to expand the food choices of their child, so that he or she accepts new, more nutritious foods. However, the success of this approach has been shown to be limited.

This article describes an alternative approach to help autistic children who are picky eaters to get more good nutrients. This approach is based in the graded, stealth introduction of key ingredients in the foods the child already accepts. These ingredients have low or null taste, so the nature of the meals the child already accepts remains unchanged or minimally modified. In this way, the nutritional value of these dishes increase in an unnoticed way. I believe that this approach minimizes the risk of rejection by the child, and also minimizes the stress to the parents that are preparing the meals. The tax to this approach is that the parents and guardians will need to learn a few things about what ingredients are important to maintain a good health, and more importantly, why they are important. The extra prize is that they can apply this knowledge to themselves and their own nutrition. Needless to say, all this is also applicable to neurotypical children (children that are not autistic).

I am taking this approach to improve nutrition in autistic children as part of a research project with Dr Anne Graham Cagney, a cognitive psychologist specialised in translating research knowledge into education, practice and policy. On my side, I am a biochemist, uncle of two autistic children who found a gap that needs to be filled. The approach presented above has surely been followed by some parents already, but in our project we want to be methodical, listen to all parties, and create materials that parents and guardians can follow to learn the basis for what they are doing, and to get ideas to implement so that their children achieve the best diet possible with the minimal burden possible.

This article will be updated over time, as I add new cases of ingredients that we need to focus on and I find stealth ways of introducing them into the diet of your child. If you have ideas or suggestions that can help this project, you can write to me at alfonso.prado-cabrero@setu.ie.

## The first case, Omega-3s

Let's go with omega-3s. I am sure you have heard things such as "your child needs fish oil for brain development" or "we don't produce enough DHA in our body". Okay, let's face two facts here. First, the dominant message that "humans do not produce enough DHA" has been scientifically contested. The second fact is that, if you are not lucky enough to have your child eating oily fish such as sardines, mackerel or salmon, making him or her take fish oil capsules is not an easy or even a recommendable task. Even without taking into account the risks of children taking capsules, fish oil capsules produce an unpleasant "fishy burping" effect that your child might not accept. Not even capsules with omega-3s obtained from algae will fully eliminate the "fishy burping".

Then, what can we do? I propose to follow the scientists who argue that our body produces enough DHA for our brain. The main argument of these scientists to defend this position is that the main reservoir of the DHA produced by our body is not the blood, but other tissues such as our fat, and that this DHA is wisely and slowly sent to the brain at the rate it needs. Well, now you might say: "Great, we don't need to do anything!" No, not quite like that. There is an essential brick that our body needs us to eat in order to produce DHA. It is alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), [the essential omega-3](/which-omega-3s-are-essential/). Such a complicated name, you would need to practise saying it and remembering it. This is the truly essential fatty acid that your child needs to consume on a regular basis, so that his or her liver produces enough DHA to supply his or her brain.

And how do we do that? Have I been providing enough alpha-linolenic acid to my child? I will answer this one first: if your child eats processed foods such as nuggets or other fried foods, the oils used in processing these foods contain some alpha-linolenic acid that has most likely kept your child producing some DHA. But what if you increase the intake of alpha-linolenic acid in your child? That would be helping him or her to produce more DHA for his or her brain. There is a way to do this without your child noticing, and here comes the answer to "How do we do that?". Some vegetable cooking oils are rich in alpha-linolenic acid (let's call it ALA from now on). The oil richest in ALA is linseed oil. However, the bitterness of this oil makes it unusable for our purpose. Another oil rich in ALA is chia oil, but this one is expensive and hard to source. Then we have canola oil. 10% of its composition is ALA. A good start. I have found brands of canola oil in Ireland that are filtered to remove its nutty flavour. Those are the brands you are looking for. If your child eats a meal such as a stew, to which you add some cooking oil, you can replace a portion of that oil with a portion of filtered canola oil. You can start with a small substitution and increase it as long as you see your child eating happily. By the way, keep the canola oil in the fridge.

## Personalisation

As you have seen from the omega-3s case above, your child's case might not fit what you read above: perhaps you are already using canola oil regularly, or your child does not eat stew at all, etc. This is why, by knowing as many personal cases as possible, we can prepare educational materials detailed enough to help as many families as possible. This is what our project is about.
